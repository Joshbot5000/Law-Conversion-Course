# Problem-question skeleton: homicide

> **ELI5:** Work from the most serious offence down. Try murder first; if the mens rea for murder is there, check the partial defences that reduce it to manslaughter; if not, go to involuntary manslaughter.

## Step 1 — Murder
- **Actus reus:** unlawful killing of a human being under the King's peace.
- **Causation:** factual (*White*) and legal (*Smith*, *Pagett*, *Blaue*, *Kennedy (No 2)*).
- **Mens rea (malice aforethought):** intention to kill or to cause GBH. Oblique intention: *Woollin* virtual certainty direction.

## Step 2 — Partial defences (only if murder is made out)
### Loss of control — Coroners and Justice Act 2009, ss 54–55
1. D lost self-control (need not be sudden).
2. A **qualifying trigger**: fear of serious violence, and/or things said or done of an extremely grave character causing D a justifiable sense of being seriously wronged.
3. A person of D's sex and age, with a normal degree of tolerance and self-restraint and in D's circumstances, might have reacted in the same or a similar way.
- Excluded where D acted in a considered desire for revenge (s 54(4)).
- **Application / conclusion:** [...]

### Diminished responsibility — Homicide Act 1957, s 2 (as amended by Coroners and Justice Act 2009, s 52)
1. An abnormality of mental functioning,
2. arising from a recognised medical condition,
3. which substantially impaired D's ability to understand the nature of their conduct, form a rational judgment, or exercise self-control,
4. and provides an explanation for D's conduct (a significant contributory factor).
- Burden on D, on the balance of probabilities.

## Step 3 — Involuntary manslaughter (if no mens rea for murder)
### Unlawful act manslaughter
1. An unlawful (criminal) act,
2. which is dangerous: sober and reasonable people would recognise it carries some risk of some harm (*R v Church* [1966]),
3. which caused death.

### Gross negligence manslaughter (*R v Adomako* [1995])
1. Duty of care owed to the victim,
2. breached,
3. causing death,
4. and the breach was so bad as to be **gross**, i.e. criminal in the jury's view.
